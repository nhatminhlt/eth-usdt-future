#### A Very Brief History of FX

#### Historical Background of Currency Markets

Currency trading has ancient roots, dating back to biblical times with money changers. The Medici family was among early foreign exchange traders in the 1400s. The modern monetary system began to shape in the early 1800s with the adoption of bimetallic and gold standards by various countries. While the gold standard engendered confidence in a currency, it was inflexible and required government control of gold ownership. By the early 20th century, the challenges of the gold standard led to the Bretton Woods system in 1944, fixing currencies to the US dollar and establishing the IMF and World Bank. This system collapsed in 1971, prompting the shift to floating exchange rates and the current fiat currency system where value is determined by market

perception.

#### Evolution of Currency Trading

Currency trading has transformed significantly since the 1970s, shaped by technology and regulation across four primary eras:

1. **The Telex Era (1971 to 1981)**
- Currency trading was executed mainly via telex and phones, primarily by banks for high-net-worth clients.
- Currency futures began trading in 1972, allowing non-bank entities to engage in currency markets.
2. **The Direct Dealing Era (1981 to 1992)**
- The introduction of a computerized dealing monitor service by Reuters in 1981 enhanced trading efficiency.
- Human brokers were still essential, but banks began to distribute risks by breaking large trades into smaller units among multiple banks.
3. **The Electronic Era (1992 to 2001)**

- The launch of online trading platforms like Dealing 2000 marked a shift toward electronic trading, enhancing speed and pricing.
- Human voice brokers decreased significantly as electronic trading gained dominance.
4. **The Algo Era (2001 to Present)**
- Algorithms (algos) developed by hedge funds transformed trading dynamics, executing trades based on various strategies.
- Retail forex trading emerged, allowing individual traders to participate with lower capital and leverage, although risks were highlighted by significant market events.
#### Algorithmic Strategies in FX Trading

Several types of algorithms are prevalent in the FX market today, including: - **Data Trading** : Instantaneous trading based on economic data releases. -

#### Market Making

: Algos create bids and offers to capture spreads. - **Correlation Trading** : Trades based on movements in correlated markets. - **Arbitrage** : Exploiting price disparities across venues. - **Trend Following and Mean Reversion** : Trading strategies based on market trends and price reversals. - **Gamma Trading** : Engaging with options and trading gamma to generate income.

Understanding the evolution of the global monetary system and the history of currency trading, along with the representation of fiat money and types of algorithms in the market, provides a solid foundation for deeper exploration of currency trading.

## Chapter 2 Summary : Currency Trading Basics

#### Chapter 3: Currency Trading Basics

#### Overview of Currency Trading

Understanding the fundamentals of currency trading is crucial for success in the foreign exchange (FX) market, which is the largest financial market, with an average turnover of $5.1 trillion per day as recorded in 2016. The section highlights the importance of being knowledgeable regarding market structure, terminology, and order types.

#### Spot vs. Forward FX Transactions

- **Spot FX** : Involves immediate settlement within 1-2 days without interest rate impacts. - **Forward FX** : Transactions are settled at a future date and are influenced by interest rates.

#### Currency Pair Conventions

Each currency pair has a numerator and a denominator. Proper quoting conventions help avoid confusion, with standard ranks assigned to currencies, prioritizing the Euro (EUR) and ranking down to the Japanese Yen (JPY).

#### Long vs. Short Positions

- **Long** : Buying a currency expecting it will increase in value. -

#### Short

: Selling a currency you don't own expecting it will decrease in value. Understanding trade direction is essential, especially with non-USD denominated trades.

#### Understanding Different Currency Pairs

The FX market features a variety of currency pairs: - **Highly Liquid Pairs** : EURUSD, GBPUSD, USDJPY, AUDUSD. - **Less Liquid Pairs** : EURJPY, USDCHF, NZDUSD. - **Illiquid Pairs** : USDRUB, XX, where trading is not recommended for short-term traders.

#### Quotes, Spreads, and Market Structure

The market operates via retail and wholesale segments, and spreads (the difference between bid and offer rates) vary

according to market conditions. The term "pip" represents the smallest trading increment, and spreads can have significant impacts on profitability.

#### Order Types

Various order types include: - **Risk Price** : Contains no risk; you buy/sell at current market rates. - **Limit Orders** : Set to execute at a specific price or better. - **Stop Loss Orders** : Automatically close trades to limit losses. - **Market Orders** : Executed at the best prevailing price. - **TWAP and VWAP** : Algorithms designed for large transactions to minimize market impact.

#### Understanding Profit and Loss in FX

Profit and loss calculations are essential for monitoring trading performance. These are determined based on price changes and position sizes, and P&L is reported in the currency of the denominator in a currency pair.

#### The Roll (Rollover)

The roll is the interest payment or receipt for holding positions overnight, influenced by interest rate differentials. It’s vital for longer-term traders but generally less significant for short-term traders.

- Stick to proper terminology and quoting conventions.
- Understand the implications of being long or short.
- Familiarize with market structure, order types, and P&L calculations to effectively navigate the FX market. This chapter emphasizes the importance of grasping these fundamentals to communicate effectively and trade competently in the currency market.

## Chapter 8 Summary : Understand Behavioral Finance

#### Chapter 9: Understand Behavioral Finance

#### Introduction

The traditional view of finance assumes rational self-interest governs economic behavior, aligning with the theory of efficient markets. However, real financial markets reveal a contrasting reality where human irrationality is prevalent. Behavioral finance explores these irrational behaviors, helping traders better understand market dynamics and improve their trading strategies.

#### Positioning and Sentiment

Traders must distinguish between sentiment (market perception) and positioning (actual market positions), as both can influence price movements differently. While sentiment can change rapidly, positioning reflects actual trades and may

lag behind sentiment shifts. Understanding these concepts allows traders to identify market extremes and better anticipate potential reversals.

#### Cognitive Bias

Awareness of cognitive biases is essential for traders. These biases include: - **Confirmation Bias** : Preference for information that supports pre-existing beliefs. - **Overconfidence Bias** : Overestimating one's abilities and potential for success. - **Extrapolation Bias** : Assuming that current trends will continue indefinitely. - **Asymmetric Loss Aversion** : Fear of losses outweighs the joy of gains, leading to suboptimal trading decisions. - **Emotional Influences**

: Greed and fear can drive market behaviors away from rationality. - **Anchoring** : Difficulty in adjusting views based on initial reference points. - **Round Number Bias** : Preference for round numbers influences market behavior. - **Favorite/Longshot Bias** : Tendency to overvalue longshot trades while underestimating safer options. - **Herding** : Following the majority to avoid personal risk, rather than independent analysis.

#### Conclusion

Understanding behavioral finance equips traders to recognize and anticipate irrational behaviors in themselves and others, leading to better trading outcomes. The chapter encourages traders to embrace the complexities of market psychology

and utilize both positioning and sentiment analysis, fortified by awareness of their cognitive biases.

## Chapter 11 Summary : Understand Position Sizing

#### Understand Position Sizing

#### Position Size as a Percentage of Capital

Position sizing is crucial in trading and depends on four main factors: available free capital, confidence in the trade, volatility of the currency pair, and stop loss placement. A systematic approach involves rating trades from Three-Star to Five-Star and allocating risk accordingly: 1%, 3%, and 6% of adjusted free capital, respectively.

#### Adjusted Free Capital

Adjusted free capital refers to the capital available for risk after accounting for year-to-date P&L. This method allows traders to remain aggressive on high-conviction trades when in a strong capital position, while conservative when losing.

#### Dynamic Risk Management

Risk allocations should fluctuate with trading success. Traders should take larger risks when profitable and decrease their exposure during losing streaks, preventing the risk of ruin. Additionally, never risk more than a certain percentage of free capital at one time, typically suggested around 3-5%.

#### Volatility and Position Size

Position sizes need to be adjusted based on the volatility of different currencies. Smaller positions should be taken in volatile pairs, ensuring that stop-loss levels are set logically based on daily ranges.

#### Identifying Five-Star Trades

A Five-Star trade occurs when multiple analysis indicators align, indicating high probability. Key factors include fundamental shifts, cross-market signals, market positioning, technical indicators, and gut feelings.

#### Limitations on Position Size

Traders often misjudge position sizes, either risking too much or too little. The focus should be on proper sizing relative to the trade's volatility and one’s conviction level. Striking a balance is essential to navigate market noise effectively.

#### Importance of Stop Losses

Stop losses are integral for risk management, setting a definite cap on potential losses. They should be determined using technical analysis, day range, and trailing mechanisms to protect profits while allowing flexibility.

#### Understanding Risk/Reward Relationship

The risk/reward ratio must reflect expected probabilities rather than fixed values. Traders should understand that risk can be greater if the probability of achieving the reward is favorable.

#### Common Errors in Position Sizing

1. **Positions Too Big**

: Leading to emotional reactions and stopping out prematurely.

2. **Positions Too Small** : Resulting in chronic underperformance and inability to cover operational costs.
3. **Fixed Position Size** : Ignoring market volatility and conviction. Proper sizing should vary with conditions.
#### Process vs. Outcome

Recognizing the difference between a well-executed process and the result of a trade is vital. Traders control their processes not outcomes, emphasizing the importance of disciplined adherence to trading rules to build long-term success.

#### Conclusion

Successful trading hinges significantly on self-awareness, discipline, and understanding psychological factors alongside market dynamics. The real battle often lies within oneself.
